# Fabrications

## Hallucination Treasure Hunt

Instead of only warning that AI systems can fabricate information, challenge
students to find a confident false answer. Possible categories include history,
local information, obscure books, invented people, science, sports, and school
information.

Students record:

> Claim / AI answer / Why I was suspicious / Evidence / Verdict

The key observation is:

> Good writing does not guarantee correct information.

“Plausibility machine” may be a useful temporary mental model: the system
produces plausible continuations, not guaranteed truth. It must not be
presented as a complete technical definition.

## Fact-Check Race

Give each group an AI-generated paragraph containing supported, false, and
misleading claims. Teams classify each claim and provide evidence:

- supported;
- false;
- misleading or not established.

Compare different sources, such as an official institution, a manufacturer, a
newspaper, Wikipedia, a forum, and another chatbot. Ask explicitly:

> Is asking another AI whether something is true a fact check?

## AI Telephone

Start with a precise description and transform it repeatedly:

> summarize → translate → expand → simplify → turn into dialogue → summarize

Compare the final version with the original. Mark information that was lost,
changed, or invented. This demonstrates that transformations are not
necessarily information-preserving.

## Can AI Know This?

Students classify questions before asking an AI system:

- probably knowable;
- requires external or current information;
- prediction;
- requires private information;
- subjective;
- impossible to know.

Example questions:

- What is the capital of Japan?
- What did I eat yesterday?
- What is inside my teacher's backpack?
- What will Bitcoin cost next year?
- How does photosynthesis work?
- Why did Julia stop talking to me?
- What is happening in Berlin right now?

The important observation is that an AI system may produce an answer even when
it cannot have the required information.
